## What it is

From the mid-1980s, when video recorders reached Ghana, mobile cinema and video-club operators travelled with a television, a VCR, a generator and cassettes, and screened action, horror, kung fu, Hollywood, Bollywood and West African films in towns and villages without theatres. To announce a screening they commissioned one-off painted posters from local sign painters, on cloth made from used flour sacks sewn together. Printing presses were rare and expensive, so the poster was a unique object. Painters often worked from a cassette cover, or, per the Beyond the Streets text, without having seen the film at all, and invented the picture. The result sits between advertisement and fine art, which is how it later reached galleries.

This is a living tradition of working painters. Sources name a Golden Age of mid-1980s to about 2000, after which digital printing arrived and the work moved towards international art markets, with the Chicago/Accra-based Deadly Prey Gallery commissioning posters since the 2010s (Wikipedia; Harman Projects says the gallery has worked with Ghanaian painters for 13 years).

## The rules that make it this and not something else

- Support: cotton flour-sack cloth, sewn to size, carried rolled on a dowel (Mubi; Wikipedia: tightly woven, takes oil and acrylic, weather-resistant). Paints named across sources: oil, enamel, acrylic.
- The painter reinterprets; the film is a prompt. Sources describe oversized weapons, surreal monsters, anatomical exaggeration and "departed radically from their source material".
- Two named regional manners (Wikipedia): coastal Accra, associated with Joe Mensah and Alex Boateng, "rippling musculature and clothing, as well as extravagant background images and explosions"; inland Kumasi, an airbrush style of macro-scale detail (Africatta, Babs). Mubi credits D.A. Jasper, a bodybuilder, with the muscles-on-muscles look.
- Each painter has a personal hand, and often a pen name (Kofi Kuwornu is "Death is Wonder", Edward Lamptey "Leonardo", E.A. Jeaurs Oko Afutu "Heavy J", D.A. Obeng "Bright Obeng", per Mubi). Credit them by name when you cite the look.
- Scale by importance: figures are not in perspective with one another; the hero, the villain and the monster each take the size the story gives them.
- The cloth shows: seams, weave, creases and cracked paint ("the patina of medieval icons", Mubi).
- Lettering is painted, bold and part of the picture.

## Tokens decoded

- Canvas: warm unbleached sack tone, proposed #c9b38a, with a 6-8 px visible weave and a stitched seam line about a third and two thirds across.
- Palette (all proposed, not sourced): black #1b1410 for outline and shadow, blood red #c4161c, hot yellow #f2c230, flesh #e8b48a, plus one full-chroma cyan or green for the monster. Keep five hues at most per poster.
- Type: Bowlby One for titles, Anton for taglines (both listed on Google Fonts; family pages confirmed this session by title only). Paint them with a 4 px dark stroke and a hard 6 px offset shadow, and rotate a title -2 to +3 degrees.
- Layout: one tall sheet, a central hero 55-70 percent of height, three to six secondary scenes (vignettes with their own small light) behind and beside.
- Texture: weave tile 512 px, crackle lines multiplied at 12-18 percent, a darker band at folds.

## Motion and camera

2D. Treat the poster as a painting that is painted in front of the viewer.
- Ground: the sack cloth at t = 0, slight push scale 1.00 to 1.05 over the scene, `sine.inOut`.
- Figures enter by layer, back to front, 90 ms stagger: opacity 0 to 1 and scale 0.9 to 1 with `back.out(1.7)` in 260 ms, with a 4-frame shake (6 px, `power4.out`) on each hit.
- The title is painted on with a brush-edge mask wipe, 500 ms `power3.out`, then a hard shadow drops in 120 ms behind it.
- Hold the finished poster 800-1200 ms before the next beat. Edits cut hard; no soft cross-dissolves.
- Cuts between posters at music hits; each next poster pushes from a different scale.

3D reading. Layered painted planes in a dark room, as a mobile cinema screen would have been hung: use `k.pxPlane` for the sack as one large plane and four to six cut-out planes (hero, monster, explosion, title) 0.05-0.4 m in front of it. `k.material("paper", {color})` or `matte` on the cut-outs so they take light but not shine; `k.rig("window")` with `dir` [-1, 0.6, 1] as a single motivated key; lens 50 mm for the first read, a 35-85 mm truck of 0.4-0.8 m so the layers separate by parallax, `power3.inOut`, 3-4 s. A hand-held 1-2 px sway is allowed only as a keyed function of `t`. Do not rotate the poster on a turntable.

## How to instruct a model to build it

> HyperFrames, 1920x1080, 30 fps, GSAP only on one paused timeline. Build a hand-painted Ghanaian video-club poster look. Ground: a unbleached sack-cloth texture tile (512 px woven pattern, #c9b38a, proposed) with two vertical stitch seams and 14 percent crackle multiply. Place five to seven painted layers as absolutely positioned divs or SVG (hero figure, monster, explosion, two scene vignettes, title), each with a 4 px #1b1410 outline, a hard 6 px offset shadow, and flat saturated fills from [#c4161c, #f2c230, #e8b48a] plus one cyan. Title in Bowlby One uppercase with 4 px stroke, rotated -2 degrees, tagline in Anton. Sequence: ground push 1.00 to 1.05 over the whole scene (`sine.inOut`); each layer enters back to front at 90 ms stagger with scale 0.9 to 1 and opacity 0 to 1 in 260 ms on `back.out(1.7)`, plus a 4-frame 6 px camera shake on hits; title brush-wipe 500 ms `power3.out`; hold 1 s. Never use a clean vector gradient, drop-shadow blur or a modern UI card. Do not caricature the culture: the poster must read as a skilled painter's invention. Claude: plan layer depth and the beat map first. GPT: ask for explicit pixel sizes of each layer or it flattens the hierarchy.

## Blending notes

- Carries well: reinterpretation scale (hero large, villain larger), layered cut-outs, crack and weave, painted lettering, hit-and-hold timing.
- With Swiss or data styles: keep the data crisp and use the painted look for a single poster interlude; do not paint the numbers.
- With risograph or screenprint: both flatten colour, so the painted modelling (soft airbrushed shadow, Kumasi style) is what remains distinct.
- Breaks: anything glossy, anything symmetrical, thin type.
