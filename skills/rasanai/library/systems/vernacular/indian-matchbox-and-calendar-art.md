## What it is
Indian bazaar art is the printed and painted picture world of calendars, framing pictures and packaging, descended from Raja Ravi Varma's oleographs. Varma set up the Ravi Varma Fine Art Lithographic Press in Bombay in 1894 (first print, the Birth of Shakuntala, July 1894; Lakshmi and Saraswati in September 1894), moved it to Malavli near Lonavala in 1899, and sold it in 1901 to the German technician Fritz Schleicher, who had come to help set it up. The prints, mostly gods and goddesses from the Mahabharata, Ramayana and Puranas, continued after his death in 1906 and, in the Google Arts & Culture account, put divine pictures in every Hindu home across caste and class lines. The press also made postcards, textile labels and match-box labels. A fire destroyed the factory in 1972 (Wikipedia). Other artists at the press are named: M.V. Dhurandhar, G.V. Venkatesh Rao, K.S. Siddalingaswamy, C. Muddayya and C.G. Ramanujam.

Sivakasi in Tamil Nadu became the printing and match capital. The Times of News piece describes anonymous calendar artists who each year drew "god pictures" for the next year's calendar, working from sketches made in temples (photography was barred there) and painting for three to four weeks. Named painters include S. Murugakani (b. 1937, in Sivakasi from 1955, own studio from 1967), C.S. Anandan (solo from 1971, nine assistants at peak, computer drawing from 2002) and N. Tirupathi Kannan (b. 1968). Computerisation from 1987 changed the trade; only about eight or nine devotional picture artists were reported active in Tamil Nadu. The same article says calendar art recasts the old skill of temple murals through theatre, cinema and colonial perspectives.

Matchbox labels follow the same economy at palm scale. Matches were imported (Europe, Japan) until Japanese immigrants began manufacture in Calcutta in 1910; Wimco (Swedish Match) started in 1924; Sivakasi factories were in full swing by the 1930s. Early labels were plain typography on one or two tones; Indian makers moved to red, yellow and blue and to tigers, peacocks, deities, dancers with veena and tabla, nationalist symbols (charkha, flag, Bharat Mata, Gandhi) and, after 1947, maps and the Ashoka Chakra. Abirpothi notes that Raja Ravi Varma's oleographs were adapted as miniature label designs. The 1950s-1960s are named as a peak for decorative labels, and Gautam Hemmady, Shreya Katuri and Warren Dotz (author of "Light of India") are cited collectors and chroniclers. The Kalighat painting story ends here too: cheap German and Bombay oleographs ended the Kalighat bazaar trade (Wikipedia).

## The rules that make it this and not something else
- **Academic realism meets Indian iconography.** Varma synthesised European academic painting with Indian iconography (Wikipedia): modelled bodies, drapery and light, with the deity in a recognisable stance.
- **Printed in passes.** Chromolithography builds the image in colour plates; the look carries a limited colour set, flat spot areas and a fine key line.
- **Image first, text second.** A central picture, a banner with the brand name, tiny print at the base.
- **Saturated primaries.** Red, yellow, blue (Abirpothi) on cream or gold. Mid-century labels often add black and a green.
- **Frames.** Arches, scalloped borders, corner ornaments, a rule line. For devotional pictures, a shrine-like arch or a lotus base.
- **Genre mixing.** Gods share a shelf with tigers, national leaders and machines; vernacular and English type appear together.
- **Respect.** These are devotional images in daily use; a film must not use them for jokes, in disrespectful contexts or with errors in iconography. When in doubt, use secular label subjects (tigers, ships, dancers, flags).

## Tokens decoded
- Canvas: a cream or yellow ground (#f6e9c9 proposed) inside a 12 to 24 px printed border; accent red #c8281e and blue #1f4aa8 (proposed).
- Type: Rozha One for the brand name at 120 to 170 px on an arc or a banner (use SVG `textPath`); fine print in Mukta 16 to 20 px in caps with 0.08em tracking (Latin and Devanagari only; Tamil in Tamil faces with 0 tracking).
- Grid: a centred label of 3:4 or 4:3 aspect inside 16:9, flanked by empty cream field or a repeating small-label pattern (a 6 x 4 field of label tiles reads as an archive).
- Shape: arch top, scalloped corner ornaments; 1 px key line, 3 px frame line.
- Texture: 2 to 3 px registration offset between colour layers (SVG `feOffset` per layer, dx 1.5, dy 1), a faint halftone at 45 degrees, paper grain 256 px tile at 4 percent.
- Imagery: invent subjects: a tiger in a frame with a brand ribbon, a ship, a dancer; avoid reproducing any one named painter's composition.

## Motion and camera
2D:
- **Colour passes (signature).** Four layers (yellow, red, blue, black key) reveal in sequence 220 ms each, `power2.out`, with a 4 px offset that settles to 1 px by the next plate; total 900 ms. Hold 700 ms.
- **Banner unfurl.** The brand banner opens from the centre, clip-path inset 0 50% to 0 over 450 ms `power3.out`.
- **Matchbox row.** A field of labels tiles in a staggered grid, 40 ms each, rows from the top, then the camera pushes onto one label (T2 focus move, 1.0 to 2.2x, 900 ms `power3.inOut`).
- **Calendar turn.** The month sheet folds up with `rotateX(-90deg)` on a top hinge, 520 ms `power2.in`, 600 ms hold between turns.
- **Camera.** Locked and frontal; 2 to 3 percent push over 3.5 s `sine.inOut`.

3D reading (Rasan3D):
- A matchbox as a physical object at hand scale: `k.panel({ width: 0.056, height: 0.036, depth: 0.014, texture })` with the printed label on the face and `k.material("paper")` on the sides; world in metres; lens 100 mm, f/2.8 so the box fills the frame and the back melts; rig `window`, warm, `dir: [-0.6, 0.5, 0.7]`.
- Camera: a 20 degree arc over 4 s `power3.inOut` ending on a face-on pose that matches a 2D label (seam `depth-to-flat` via `k.layout({ at: 4 })`). No spin.
- Calendars as layered flat planes behind glass: `k.pxPlane` for the picture at z 0, a frame at z 0.04, glass `k.material("glass")` with an opaque `background`; reflections need the studio environment at 0.6.
