## What it is

Afrofuturism is a field of art, music, literature and thought that imagines Black futures. The critic Mark Dery named it in 1993 (the essay "Black to the Future" appears in the anthology Flame Wars, 1994, with interviews of Samuel R. Delany, Greg Tate and Tricia Rose) and defined it as "speculative fiction that treats African-American themes and addresses African-American concerns in the context of twentieth-century technoculture", and more broadly "African American signification that appropriates images of technology and a prosthetically enhanced future". The practice is older than the word: Sun Ra was staging it in the 1950s. Others have shaped the field since: Kodwo Eshun reads it through the Middle Passage and alienation, Ytasha Womack calls it "an intersection of imagination, technology, the future and liberation", and Nnedi Okorafor proposed Africanfuturism in 2019 for work rooted in African culture and viewpoint. Because it is a body of authors' work, and not a house style, this entry describes recurring visual systems and warns against flattening them.

## The rules that make it this and not something else

- It is about authorship and liberation: the future is imagined by the people it excludes in the standard story. The visual shorthand is secondary to that stance.
- Four visual families recur (the sources support each; the grouping is this entry's):
  1. Cosmic and Egyptian stagecraft. Sun Ra and the Arkestra wore Egyptian and space-age costumes from the late 1950s. He gave reasons: fascination with ancient Egypt and the space age, band unity, new onstage identities, and humour (Wikipedia). His own record label, El Saturn, was run with Alton Abraham from the mid-1950s. The 1974 film Space Is the Place has him return to Chicago to take Black Americans to a new planet by music.
  2. P-Funk marker maximalism. Pedro Bell (1950-2019, Chicago) made covers for Funkadelic and George Clinton from 1973 (Cosmic Slop) through the 1980s and 90s (Standing on the Verge of Getting It On 1974, One Nation Under a Groove 1978, Uncle Jam Wants You 1979, The Electric Spanking of War Babies 1981, Computer Games 1982). He worked in markers and felt-tip pens at 300 percent of cover size, often traced with acrylic because of colour separation in print (Wikipedia). His influences named there include Dali, Ed Roth and Robert Williams. Dense, comic, cosmic, with his own liner-note mythology.
  3. Collage-hybrid bodies. Wangechi Mutu (born Nairobi 1972, Yale MFA 2000) works with magazine clippings, ink, mica flakes, polymer paint and found materials, and constructs hybrid human-machine-alien figures. The Wikipedia article places her work in Afrofuturism and Africanfuturism.
  4. Contemporary Afrofuturist and Africanfuturist cinema and music. Janelle Monae uses cyborg aesthetics and narrative worlds (Wikipedia). Wanuri Kahiu's short Pumzi (2009, Sundance 2010) imagines an underground post-water-war community; she co-founded the Afrobubblegum collective, supporting "fun, fierce and frivolous" African art (search summary) and "African art for its own sake" (Wikipedia). Her later work includes Rafiki (2018).
- The look of each is built from material (marker, collage, costume), not from a render effect.
- Space and technology are owned, not borrowed: figures pilot the ship; they are not rescued by it.
- Water, the Middle Passage and return are recurring themes (Wikipedia names water symbolism); handle them with specificity or leave them out.

## Tokens decoded

- Measured from the 1974 Space Is the Place poster: gold `#c5a548`, ochre `#8e5c3c`, aged paper `#ccbfa5`, dark `#494741`: the Sun Ra look at source is earth-and-gold on cream, not neon. Proposed screen set: night `#0b0a1f`, ink `#f4efe2`, gold `#f2b705`, violet `#7b2cbf`, red `#e63946`, teal `#0fb5ae`. Skin tones painted warm and true, never grey-blue. A film may take either the measured poster palette (period, warm) or the proposed night set (cosmic stage): say which.
- Cosmic branch: gold-and-violet on night, a centred figure inside a circle, 60 percent of frame height, small star-dot field at 3 percent opacity.
- P-Funk branch: 3 px marker outlines in 3-4 saturated colours on a dense field; 10-16 small vignettes plus a central figure; lettering irregular.
- Collage branch: cut-out edges visible, a 2 px paper edge shadow, mica fleck sparkle only in small areas.
- Contemporary branch: smooth, with film grain 0.02, high key colour pops against a dark ground.
- Type: Syne 800 for titles and Space Grotesk for text; for the P-Funk branch, trace a hand-lettered treatment instead of a font if the budget allows.

## Motion and camera

2D
- Ascension: the figure rises from the bottom edge 120 px over 1200 ms on `power3.out`; the ground drops away 8 percent faster than the figure (parallax d = 0.4), the star layer 0.2.
- Circle iris: a mask radius from 0 to 90 percent in 600 ms on `power2.inOut`, to open on a scene as if a portal.
- Collage assemble: pieces enter from their nearest edges, 80 ms stagger, 300 ms `power3.out`, each with a 2 px rotation settling to 0.
- Dense P-Funk surfaces: a slow pan across the sheet (3-5 s, `sine.inOut`) that reveals vignettes, never a scale-up of everything at once.
- Hold 1200 ms on a finished composition; sound carries the rest.

3D reading (Rasan3D; real API, parameters proposed). A stage and a ship, as places: a backdrop of painted cosmos on `k.pxPlane(group,{x:0,y:0,w:1920,h:1080,texture:k.image("assets/cosmos.png")})` placed at the plane the camera sees (`k.layout({at:0,distance:30})`), a circular riser from `CylinderGeometry(3,3,0.2,64)` in `k.material("matte",{color:"#494741"})` with a gold ring `TorusGeometry` in `k.material("emissive",{color:"#f2b705"})` (emissive is the only thing that blooms; bloom `{strength:0.4,threshold:1.15}`), a figure as a cut-out plane (`k.panel` or `PlaneGeometry` with a transparent texture) or a GLB with `matte`. Light `k.rig("rim",{key:"#ffd27a",rim:"#7b2cbf",fill:"#1a1440",dir:[-0.6,0.9,0.7],intensity:1,shadows:true})`; `environment:"soft"`; `fog:{color:"#0b0a1f",near:6,far:40}`. Camera keys: `lens:[[0,35]]` for the stage; departure as `pos:[[0,[0,1.2,12]],[3.2,[0,9,10],"power3.inOut"]]` with `lens:[[0,35],[3.2,24,"power3.inOut"]]` and a 0.3s hold at the top; `target` follows the figure upward. Chrome only where the story is about technology. Never a lone orbiting spaceship in a void.

## How to instruct a model to build it

> HyperFrames, 1920x1080, 30 fps, GSAP, one paused timeline. Build an Afrofuturist film language that has a point of view: someone is imagining their own future. Pick one family per scene: (a) cosmic stage: night ground #0b0a1f (proposed), a centred circle, a figure 60 percent of frame height, gold #f2b705 and violet #7b2cbf, a 3 percent star-dot layer; (b) marker maximalism: a dense field of 10-16 small vignettes with 3 px marker outlines in 3-4 saturated colours, irregular hand lettering; (c) collage: cut-out pieces with visible 2 px paper edges. Motion: figures rise 120 px over 1200 ms `power3.out` with parallax layers at d = 0.4 and 0.2; a circle iris opens in 600 ms `power2.inOut`; collage pieces enter at 80 ms stagger. Type in Syne 800 and Space Grotesk. Name your invented places and beings yourself. Do not use neon grids, chrome pyramids, ankhs or Adinkra as decoration. Claude: write the one-sentence stance ('who imagines this future, and what do they keep?') before building. GPT: forbid cyberpunk neon and generic starfields explicitly.

## Blending notes

- Carries: authorship stance, ascent as a structure, material-first surfaces (marker, collage, costume), a dense, joyful maximalism where the story allows.
- With psychedelic poster or hand-painted vernacular: strong fit through hand lettering and all-over fill.
- With Swiss or minimal: keep a single cosmic circle and gold on night.
- Breaks: gloss, cold sci-fi interface chrome, any look that treats a Black figure as an extra in someone else's future.
Also blends with (not library entries): collage; hand-painted-vernacular.
